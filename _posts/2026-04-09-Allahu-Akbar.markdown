I would describe myself as an agnostic—someone who isn’t certain whether God exists or not. To me, the existence of God remains one of the greatest mysteries. Billions of people across the world follow different faiths, each convinced of their own version of truth. But I often question this idea of belief itself.

Osho once said, “Belief is the most poisonous thing for human intelligence,” because the moment you believe in something absolutely, you stop questioning it. And thats where my discomfort with organized religion begins. Most religions are built on belief systems handed down by prophets or scriptures, and people seem to follow them with surprising ease.

What I find strange is this: we struggle to fully understand something like quantum physics—something real, observable, and deeply comple,yet many claim to understand God so clearly. That certainty feels misplaced to me.

For me, God is not a fixed definition, not a question that needs a rigid answer. It shouldn’t be for anyone. Every individual should have the freedom to discover what God means to them. You might find God in nature—in trees, water, the sun. You might find it in your parents, or within yourself. Why must it be confined to descriptions written in ancient texts or defined by prophets?

I don’t see God as a figure described by religious authorities. To me, God is something far greater—something closer to what Gautama Buddha represents in spirit: awareness, stillness, compassion. Not a being that judges or punishes, but something vast, silent, joyful, and deeply alive.

I’ve often reflected on practices across religions. For example, in Islam, followers bow only to Allah. While I understand the devotion behind it, I personally see beauty in humility toward all of creation. Bowing to the sun, to water, to a tree, or even to a stone—these acts can symbolize respect for the universe and a recognition that we are not above anything else.

If everything is created by God, then respecting creation is, in a way, respecting God. A God that becomes angry at such humility feels limited—more like a human projection than something infinite.
